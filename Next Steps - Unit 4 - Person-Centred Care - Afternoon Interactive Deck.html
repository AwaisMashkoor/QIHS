Person-centred care finds the balance between the two — with him, not for him.

The case tells us he is finding the changes to his diet hard. It does not tell us what food means to him — so we don’t write it in.

The most useful thinking tool in person-centred care: separate what is important to someone from what is important for them, and find the balance. Important to is what the person says, or shows, matters to them. Important for is what others see they need to be healthy and safe. For a long time services focused almost entirely on important for. Point at how short the first column is: the case gives us one thing Richard wants, in his own words. Everything else about what matters to him would be invented — our habit again, and the handbook says not to add issues to the case. Then the balance: living at home (to) and a warm, dry home (for) pull the same way — that is where support starts. Medication (for) is harder: how do we make it fit a life he wants? That can only be answered with him — which is where the afternoon is going. Don’t list the questions here: after the break the groups write their own (examples are in the debrief panel).

Having schizophrenia doesn’t mean Richard can’t decide things for himself. Ability is judged decision by decision, at the time — so choose the time well, and support him to take part.

Someone on his side

Scotland’s mental health law gives him a right to independent advocacy — help to have his say, from someone independent of the NHS and the council. The wellbeing hub in his case helps with advocacy.

Questions anyone can use in a decision:

BWhat are the Benefits?RWhat are the Risks?AWhat are the Alternatives?NWhat if I do Nothing?

Three practical things, no legal lecture. Ask on a good day: diagnosis is not incapacity — decision-making ability is considered for the particular decision and circumstances, and support should maximise the person’s involvement; the Standards say people’s views should still be sought and choices respected. Does planning with people work?

What the evidence says

A review of 19 trials, with 10,856 people, found personalised care planning probably led to small improvements in some physical measures, probably reduced symptoms of depression, and improved people’s confidence and skills to manage their health. No harms were found.

It worked best when it included preparation, record-sharing, care coordination and review — and was built into routine care.

The effects were small, and the evidence of moderate quality.

Coulter A et al. (2015) Personalised care planning for adults with chronic or long-term health conditions. Cochrane Review.

Step three: make it last. The evidence, described carefully. It is a 2015 Cochrane review, not new, and the effects are small: 19 trials, 10,856 people; personalised care planning probably gave small improvements in some physical measures (such as blood sugar and blood pressure in diabetes), probably reduced symptoms of depression, and improved people’s confidence and skills to manage their own health — the 8,757 hours from Unit 2. No harms were found. The reason to use it is the last finding: it worked best when it included preparation, record-sharing, care coordination and review, and was built into routine care. Then the right card — this morning’s questions pointed at his answers: where would they live, who should see them (appropriate sharing — should the housing officer see what he finds hard about his food?), who keeps them up to date, what happens when he leaves the ward? A plan in a drawer changes nothing; the centre needs the roof. For the leaflet: this is what person-centred care can achieve, in plain words.

Richard’s conversation — debrief

1 · What did you know?

Mostly what matters for him: his medication, his blood sugar, a warm dry home, his tenancy. In his own words, one thing: he wants to live at home, independently.

2 · Your best question

Each group reads out one. Is it open? Is it about his life, not just his illness? Questions groups often write: What does a good day look like? What do you enjoy? What is hard about the changes to your food? How would you like your daughter involved? What worries you about going home?

3 · Helping him take part

A good day and a good time. Somewhere he’s comfortable. Someone on his side — the hub helps with advocacy. It’s OK to ask: benefits, risks, alternatives, nothing.

4 · His daughter’s card

Her own conversation, not a box on his plan. What would you ask her? What support might she need — and has anyone offered her a carer support plan?

The point to land: the case can’t tell you what matters to Richard. Only Richard can. Your questions are the start of the centre of the house — and his answers, in his words, become his one-page profile.

Glossary — the centre

Plain English, one line each.

See the person

  • Person-centred care — doing things with people, not to them or just for them, built around what matters to them.
  • Two experts — the professional, with knowledge and skill, and the person, the expert in their own life.
  • Values-based approach — care guided by values: dignity and respect, compassion, being included, responsive care and support, wellbeing.
  • Important to / important for — what the person says or shows matters to them, and what others see they need to be healthy and safe.
  • Unpaid carer — a family member or friend who provides care without being paid.
  • Adult carer support plan — a plan about a carer’s own needs and outcomes, which the council must offer to an identified adult carer.

Ask the person · make it last

  • Care and support planning — the centre of the house: get ready, talk about what matters, agree a plan together.
  • One-page profile — a single page, in the person’s words: what people like about them, what’s important to them, how best to support them.
  • Open question — a question that starts with what, how or tell me, and can’t be answered yes or no.
  • Capacity — the ability to make a particular decision at a particular time. A diagnosis doesn’t decide it.
  • Independent advocacy — support from someone independent of services, to help a person have their say.
  • BRAN — Benefits, Risks, Alternatives, what if I do Nothing: questions for making a decision together.
  • Personalised care planning — the research name for planning care with people: agreeing goals and actions together.
  • Digital exclusion — not being able to use online services because of cost, access, skills or confidence.
