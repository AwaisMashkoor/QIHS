Year of Care; IPPR

Year of Care Partnerships
If we only get three hours, the job isn't to fix people in the room. It's to make the other 8,757 hours work.

This is the House of Care's own founding fact and the reason it looks the way it does. Three hours is not enough time to manage anyone's condition for them — so the house is built to prepare the person for the other 8,757 hours: information before the appointment, a conversation about what matters, a plan they own, and support outside the clinic. Unit 2Why a house and not a list
What happens when a part is missing?

Take a wall away. What happens to Richard?

The point of a house

Every part depends on the others

You can have four parts out of five and still have no shelter.

This is why the metaphor is a house rather than a checklist. Remove the roof and the best conversation in the world gets lost because nothing records it and nobody else knows it happened. Remove the left wall and you have a professional talking at someone who has not been prepared for the conversation. Remove the foundation and the plan is medically perfect and practically impossible — no housing officer, no daughter, no hub. Let students call out the consequence before you click; the panel is there to confirm, not to teach. Ask, at the end: which part of Richard's house was missing this morning? Most will say the roof or the foundation. Both are right.

Unit 2True or false
Hands up · then we check

Four things people say about the house

"Person-centred care always takes longer."
False. The first planning conversation is longer. The next twenty appointments are shorter, because the person arrives prepared and the plan already exists.
True or false?
"Shared care plans are only for GPs."
False. The hospital, the community team, the social worker and the person all use the same plan. That is the point of the roof.
True or false?
"Patients don't want the responsibility."
False. People don't want to be abandoned. But they don't want to be passive either. What they want is partnership — and offered a real say, most people take it.
True or false?
"It's too complex to do in real life."
False. Nobody builds the whole house on a Monday. Start with one part: one team, one question — "what matters to you?" — at the start of every appointment.
True or false?

Energiser straight after the break — five minutes, hands up for each, then click to reveal. All four are false, which the room will start to guess by the third; that's fine, ask them why. The first one matters most because it is what professionals say when they resist care planning; the answer is that time moves rather than grows. Unit 2Worked example
Maria · a weak house, and one small fix for each part

How you strengthen a house

Maria

Every part is weak. One fix at a time.

Maria, 52. COPD and type 2 diabetes. Three appointments a month at three places, eleven medicines from three doctors, and a plastic bag of paper because nobody has the whole picture. What matters to her — "I need to keep working, to support my daughter through university" — nobody has ever asked.

Each fix: three months, under £500. Root cause is not "poor communication". Success is not "feels better".

This is a demonstration, not an activity — students watch you reason. Take each weak component in turn, say out loud what the root cause is, what one small change could make a big difference, who would need to be involved and how you would know it worked. Then click the fix. The house heals visibly. Keep it to fifteen minutes and resist opening it up; they get their turn next, on Richard. The reasoning pattern you model here — root cause, small change, who, how would we know — is the pattern they follow on their own sheets.

Keep the sheet. Your December case report is the written version of what you are drawing now.

This is the main event of the day and the only group task of the afternoon; give it the full thirty minutes. Circulate constantly. The most common failure is groups filling the walls and the roof with generic phrases ("good communication") — push them back to the map: which named person, which named service, which actual boundary. The daughter is a genuinely interesting problem: she belongs on the left wall as a carer to be informed and supported, and on the foundation as informal support. Groups that notice she is in two places have understood the framework. Ten minutes before the end, tell every group to stop building and pick their weakest component and its fix.

Glossary — the words in Richard's story

Plain-English, one line each. Nobody is expected to know these already.

The organisations

  • NHS Scotland — the health service. Free at the point of use. Organised into area health boards that run hospitals, community health and mental health services.
  • Local authority / council — elected local government. Responsible for social work, social care, housing, benefits advice, schools.
  • Social care — help with daily living: washing, dressing, meals, support at home, care homes. Assessed by the council and can be charged for.
  • Third sector — charities, voluntary and community organisations. Advice, advocacy, peer support, activities.
  • Integration authority / Integration Joint Board (IJB) — the body set up in each area, under the 2014 Act, to plan health and social care together.
  • Health and Social Care Partnership (HSCP) — the local arrangement through which integrated services are delivered.

The people

  • GP — general practitioner, the family doctor. First point of contact; refers to specialists.
  • Ward team — the doctors, nurses and others looking after someone while they are in hospital.
  • Community mental health team — NHS team supporting people with mental illness at home rather than in hospital.
  • Social worker — council professional who assesses needs, arranges support and protects people at risk.
  • Housing officer — council or housing association staff who manage tenancies, rent arrears and repairs.
  • Unpaid carer — a family member or friend who provides care without being paid. Has rights to support of their own.
  • Link worker — connects people to non-medical support in the community (social prescribing).

Richard's situation

  • Long-term condition — a health condition that can be managed but not cured, lived with for years, shaping daily life.
  • Schizophrenia — a serious mental illness affecting thinking, perception and mood. Treatable; often long-term, with periods of being well and periods of relapse.
  • Type 2 diabetes — a long-term condition where the body cannot control blood sugar well; managed by diet, activity and often medication.
  • Inpatient unit / admission / discharge — a hospital ward where someone stays for treatment; being taken in; being sent home with a plan.
  • Tenancy — the legal agreement to rent a home. Rent arrears — unpaid rent that is owed. Both can put the home at risk.
  • Wellbeing hub / community wellbeing service — a local service bringing together help with housing, money, health and community activities in one place.

The ideas

  • Integrated care — health and social care planned and delivered so they work as one around the person.
  • Fragmentation — care that falls apart at the joins between services.
  • Continuity — whether information, the plan and relationships hold together over time.
  • Care plan — a written record of someone's needs, goals and the support agreed.
  • Multidisciplinary team (MDT) — professionals from different disciplines working together on one person's care.
  • Person-centred care — care organised around what matters to the person, with them as a partner. Unit 4.
  • Anticipatory care planning — planning ahead for what might happen, before it becomes a crisis. Unit 8.
  • House of Care — the framework for doing all of this. This afternoon.
