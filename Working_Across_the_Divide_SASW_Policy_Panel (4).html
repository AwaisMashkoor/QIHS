Working Across the Divide

What integration really asks of the people who do the work

Awais Mashkoor
University of the West of Scotland

The reality

Two systems. One room. No manual.

A decade ago, we put structures in place and assumed people would know how to work together. They didn't. Here's what the divide actually looks like.

The relational work nobody designed

Leaders described years of informal, unstructured work that makes integration function. It isn't funded. It isn't measured. But without it, structures are empty.

Four practical patterns stood out

⟡
Building trust across boundaries
Trust isn't built in board meetings. It's built over years of personal contact — corridor conversations, shared problems, keeping promises when nobody's watching. Some leaders invested years in cross-party relationships before they could lead effectively.

Years, not months

One leader spent years building trust with opposition members who were suspicious of their party. That investment paid off when they needed cross-party support for a difficult service decision.

Relationships before role

In smaller boards, pre-existing personal relationships drove governance more than formal structures. People knew each other — there was no anonymity.

I spent years working with them building up trust with those opposition members because some of them are quite suspicious of my party. And that time I've invested over these years, I thought it's starting to pay off.— Interviewee B, 2022
▸
Making hard calls together
When budgets are under acute pressure, leaders can't just facilitate — they have to make difficult decisions about services that communities are deeply attached to. The leaders who did this well brought communities with them rather than imposing decisions on them.

Closures carry identity

Community hospitals are places where people lost a parent, where their auntie works. Closing them isn't just a service decision — it's an identity decision. Leaders had to understand that emotional weight.

Finance drives everything

When resources don't shift, integration stalls. Leaders described competing for the same shrinking budget across two systems with different priorities.

Closures are terrible. These community hospitals are often an identity. It means so much — it's where you perhaps lose a parent, or your auntie works. It's really difficult.— Interviewee B, 2022
◆
Using local intelligence
The most effective leaders didn't rely on formal consultations to understand community needs. They went to where people already were — lunch clubs, community centres, informal gatherings — and listened. The intelligence they gathered there was richer than anything a formal appointment could produce.

Informal beats formal

People share things in a lunch club they would never share in a surgery appointment — the state of the bus service, the real barriers to care, the isolation.

Local knowledge matters

Rural leaders in particular used deep community familiarity to identify service gaps that data couldn't capture.

If you sent a district nurse to join them at lunch, you would get more intelligence from that lunch than any appointment from any doctor. They would never tell you that in a face-to-face appointment.— Interviewee E, 2022
◎
Creating safe spaces
Some leaders deliberately created environments where people felt safe to ask questions, challenge assumptions, and admit when they didn't understand something. In boards of 20 where two organisational cultures meet, that psychological safety is the precondition for everything else.

No daft questions

One leader insisted there was no such thing as a stupid question. Nine times out of ten, when someone asked the "daft" question, others around the table were thinking the same thing.

The simplest questions

The power that should be invested in every board member — especially community members — is the power to ask: what does that actually mean? Why is that important? Why isn't what I think important?

I put a lot of effort into creating an environment where people felt safe to ask questions. There's no such thing as a daft question. And the chief financial officer and the chief officer were super in terms of how they responded.— Interviewee H, 2022
Structures were never enough. Integration asks people to lead without authority, build trust across professional boundaries, and persist when the system doesn't support them. These skills can be developed.

The assumption

We put structures in place and assumed people would know how to work collaboratively. A decade on, practitioners are telling us they need help.

The reality

The relational work that makes integration function — trust-building, cultural translation, navigating competing logics — was never designed, funded, or measured.

The opportunity

We don't have more resources. But we can invest in the only flexible resource there is — human capital. Better relationships are the only way to make better use of what we have.

Continuing Professional Development

This research is now informing a CPD/PDP programme being developed through UWS, IFIC and Scottish Government, designed to support leaders working across health and social care integration. Happy to discuss further if useful.
