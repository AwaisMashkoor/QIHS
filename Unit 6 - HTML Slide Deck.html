HIV: the science works. Does the care system?

We have effective ways to prevent and treat HIV. What must health and social care organise so that people actually benefit, and what can we learn from two different settings?

The achievement

HIV can be prevented and effectively treated. Many people live long lives with HIV.

Today’s question

What must a care system organise, beyond the medicine, for that science to reach people? And how do two very different countries do it?

HIV is our case. The teaching is population effects, care systems, evidence and the response.

Topic B, in plain words

What the assessment asks, and what it does not.

The handbook question

“The burden of contagious diseases such as HIV differs widely between high- and low-income countries. Illustrate the effect that one contagious disease has on the population and care systems in one high-income and one low-income country.”

What it asks of you

  • Population: who is affected, and how is the burden distributed?
  • Care systems: what must services organise and deliver?
  • Two settings: one high-income and one low-income country.
  • Not a biology lecture: enough disease knowledge to be accurate, then analysis of people and systems.
HIV is a taught example. You may choose Topic A, B or C, and within Topic B any contagious disease and any qualifying pair of countries.

Foundations · Week 6 · 6

Five things every health and social care worker should know about HIV

Plain language. The detail is in the Sway, Part A.

1 · HIV is not AIDS

HIV is a virus that weakens the immune system. AIDS describes advanced illness if HIV has badly damaged it. Most people on effective treatment never develop AIDS.

2 · Ordinary contact is safe

HIV is not passed on through cups, hugs, toilets or everyday care.

3 · Treatment works

ART (antiretroviral therapy) is the name for the medicines that treat HIV. They lower the viral load, the amount of HIV in the blood, often until tests cannot detect it.

4 · U=U: Undetectable = Untransmittable

When treatment keeps the viral load undetectable over time, HIV is not passed on through sex. This applies to sexual transmission, not every route.

5 · Prevention is a mix

Testing, condoms, sterile injecting equipment, and two medicines: PrEP, taken before a possible exposure, and PEP, which needs urgent assessment after a possible exposure, as soon as possible and within 72 hours.

Facts 2 and 4 are different. The cup was never a risk; U=U is a separate finding about sexual transmission.
Source / evidence: WHO HIV fact sheet • 2026, NHS HIV and AIDS guidance, Rodger et al. • PARTNER 2019

Foundations · Week 6 · 7

What those facts mean for services

Knowing the science changes what services should do.

For staff in any service

  • Do not treat a person as an infection risk during ordinary care; use standard precautions for everyone.
  • Share HIV status only where it is relevant to the person’s care.
  • Use accurate language: “living with HIV”, not “AIDS patient”.

For the system

  • Prevention needs accessible information and services.
  • Treatment needs reliable medicines and continuing follow-up.
  • U=U should change stigma and professional communication.
A scientifically effective intervention still has to be reachable and trusted.
Source / evidence: WHO HIV fact sheet • 2026, HIV Informed (Waverley Care)

Burden · Week 6 · 9

What does “burden” mean in Topic B?

Two halves: what happens to people, and what services must do.

Population effects

  • People living with HIV, new infections and preventable deaths
  • Unequal patterns by group, gender, place and circumstance
  • Late diagnosis and its consequences
  • Stigma, privacy, relationships and wellbeing

Care-system effects

  • Prevention and testing
  • Treatment and continuing follow-up
  • Workforce knowledge and attitudes
  • Medicines and supply
  • Community and third-sector support
  • Funding and policy
Globally in 2025: about 41 million people were living with HIV, 1.2 million acquired it, 570,000 died of AIDS-related causes, and about 9 million living with HIV were not on treatment.
Source / evidence: UNAIDS special report • July 2026, WHO HIV fact sheet • 2026

Burden · Week 6 · 11

95–95–95: a service pathway, and what it misses

Each step counts the people who reached the one before.

WHO’s global estimate for 2025 was 88 → 89 → 95. The 1,000-person example is invented to show denominators.

Ask of any cascade

  • Who never entered the first step?
  • Who was lost between steps?
  • What does suppression measure?

What it cannot see

  • Stigma and privacy
  • Social care and home support
  • Treatment in another service, such as Derek’s doorstep
Source / evidence: WHO HIV fact sheet • 2026

Burden · Week 6 · 12

Four checks before any comparison

Place, year, population, what was counted.

Place

UK, England or Scotland? National or global?

Year

Which year, and which reporting period?

Population

All ages or 15 to 49? Everyone, or people in care?

What was counted

New infections, new diagnoses, people living with HIV, or a share?

New diagnoses and new infections

Diagnoses can rise while new infections fall. More testing finds infections acquired in earlier years. A diagnosis records when HIV was found, not when it was acquired.

Source / evidence: UKHSA surveillance • 2024 data

Compare · Week 6 · 15

Lens 1 · Population: who is affected, and how?

Different measures in each country. Unequal patterns inside both.

United Kingdom

  • 3,043 people first diagnosed with HIV in the UK in 2024 (3,169 in 2023).
  • England: 42% of adults newly diagnosed in 2024 with a qualifying CD4 result were diagnosed late.
  • Scotland: 39 of 132 first-ever diagnoses in 2025 were late.
  • England: 52% of the 103,689 people in HIV care were aged 50 or over.

Rwanda

  • 2.2% HIV prevalence among people aged 15 to 49 (DHS 2025).
  • Women 2.8% versus men 1.5%.
  • Urban 3.3% versus rural 1.7%.
  • An estimated 236,538 people living with HIV (UNAIDS 2024 estimate, in the RBC report).

What it means for people and services

  • HIV is lifelong: care continues for decades, increasingly alongside the health needs of older age.
  • A late diagnosis means starting treatment after the immune system is already damaged, and an earlier chance to test was missed.
  • Different groups carry different burdens, so one model of testing and support will not fit everyone.
Different measures in each country: describe each pattern and what it means; do not rank them.
Source / evidence: UKHSA surveillance • 2024 data, PHS Scotland • 2025 data, Rwanda DHS • 2025, Rwanda Biomedical Centre • HIV report 2024/25

Compare · Week 6 · 16

Can we put these side by side?

A comparison is only as sound as the measures used.

UK 2024 first diagnoses (3,043) versus Rwanda 2025 prevalence (2.2%)

No. Different definitions, denominators and periods, so they cannot show which country has more new infections. We would need compatible incidence estimates.

Scotland’s 2025 treatment cascade versus Rwanda’s 2024 cascade

Yes, as similar kinds of service indicator, with qualifications about year, reporting method and source.

Rwanda women’s prevalence (2.8%) versus men’s (1.5%) in the same survey

Yes. It is a within-survey contrast of the same age-bounded measure. The survey cannot explain why it occurs.

Place · year · population · what was counted. A careful refusal to rank is a strong answer.
Source / evidence: UKHSA surveillance • 2024 data, PHS Scotland • 2025 data, Rwanda DHS • 2025, Rwanda Biomedical Centre • HIV report 2024/25

Compare · Week 6 · 17

Lens 2 · Access: how do people reach prevention and testing?

A service can exist without everyone knowing about it, being offered it, trusting it or being able to use it.

United Kingdom

  • England, 2024: among people attending sexual health services who met UKHSA’s definition of PrEP need, 79.4% of white gay and bisexual men started or continued PrEP, compared with 34.6% of Black African heterosexual women.
  • Scotland: 2,395 people started PrEP for the first time in 2025.

Rwanda

  • 96% of people estimated to be living with HIV were diagnosed (2024 cascade, RBC report).
  • Our sources do not show who the remaining 4% are, or how access differs by place.

What it means for people and services

  • Availability is not access: people must know about a service, be offered it, trust it and be able to reach it.
  • How needs are recognised, how information is offered and where services sit all shape who benefits.
Source / evidence: UKHSA surveillance • 2024 data, PHS Scotland • 2025 data, Rwanda Biomedical Centre • HIV report 2024/25

Compare · Week 6 · 18

Lens 3 · Continuity: who stays in care?

Two strong reported pathways, from different systems and years.

Scotland, end of 2025

  • 94% diagnosed → 92% of those on ART (July 2024 to December 2025) → 98% of those suppressed.
  • 334 people (about 5%) had not attended specialist HIV care in the previous 18 months.

Rwanda, 2024

  • 96% diagnosed → 98% on ART → 98% suppressed (RBC report, drawing on UNAIDS estimates).
  • 65% of clients were on six-month medicine refills (RBC 2024/25).

What staying in care involves

  • Reliable medicine supplies, so people do not run out.
  • Follow-up that fits around work, travel and family life.
  • A way to reconnect people who lose contact, not only to record them as lost.
Low national income did not predict a weak cascade. A high suppression figure does not tell us who is outside it.
Source / evidence: PHS Scotland • 2025 data, Rwanda Biomedical Centre • HIV report 2024/25, WHO Africa Rwanda • 2024

Compare · Week 6 · 19

Lens 4 · Wider care and trust: beyond the HIV clinic

Derek’s problem is a wider-care problem. What do we know in each country?

United Kingdom

  • Specialist clinics rated highly, GPs less so; 13.7% worried about being treated differently by healthcare staff (Positive Voices 2022).
  • An ageing population in care has more contact with GPs, hospitals and social care.
  • Third-sector organisations such as Waverley Care and Terrence Higgins Trust.

Rwanda

  • HIV services further integrated with TB, non-communicable disease and mental health services (RBC 2024/25).
  • Peer educators and community organisations support people in care.

What support outside the clinic contributes

  • Peer supporters (people with lived experience) help others manage treatment, stigma and services.
  • Third-sector charities offer information, advocacy and staff learning.
  • Integration lets one visit cover HIV alongside other conditions.

What the sources report is delivery, not experience: our Rwanda sources do not measure stigma in wider services. The People Living with HIV Stigma Index is where you would look.

Source / evidence: Positive Voices 2022 • UKHSA, HIV Informed (Waverley Care), Terrence Higgins Trust, Rwanda Biomedical Centre • HIV report 2024/25

  • Outreach, peer support and community contact

Respond · Week 6 · 21

A service redesign: fewer journeys. Better outcomes?

Two sources with different jobs, through the Week 4 lens.

Both help us understand service redesign, but they do not evaluate the same intervention or outcome. The Kigali study compared people starting treatment in 2014 and 2017: the later group started within a week regardless of CD4 count and collected medicines every three to six months rather than every one to three.

Sebeza et al. (2025): 976 adults, 28 Kigali health centres; staying in care (retention) at 24 months 92.5%; adjusted odds ratio 1.11 (95% CI 0.67 to 1.85).

A policy promise is not an outcome

Commitment → implementation → reach → outcome.

Scotland • 2023–26

HIV Transmission Elimination Delivery Plan: commitments towards ending transmission by 2030.

England • 2025–30

HIV Action Plan: prevent, test, treat, thrive and collaborate.

Global • 2026–31

UNAIDS strategy: country leadership, equity and rights, community leadership, sustainable financing.

Name which stage your source shows. Never quote England’s plan as Scottish or UK-wide policy.

Activity · Week 6 · 25

Your task: build a Topic B argument

Not a list of figures: an argument about two care systems.

Five outputs

  1. One supported population effect in each country
  2. One care-system strength or gap in each
  3. One contextual explanation
  4. One limitation in the evidence
  5. One realistic recommendation for a named group

One worked example: PrEP in England

  • Finding: among people attending sexual health services with a PrEP need, uptake was 79.4% for white gay and bisexual men and 34.6% for Black African heterosexual women (UKHSA, 2024).
  • Meaning: making prevention available has not produced equal uptake in this service population.
  • Service question: how are needs recognised, information offered and access supported?
  • Limit: the percentages do not explain the difference or show which response would work.
Your 45-second report is your main comparative conclusion, not all five outputs read aloud; the worksheet holds the detail. You are not asked to disclose personal experience or represent your home country.
Source / evidence: UKHSA surveillance • 2024 data
