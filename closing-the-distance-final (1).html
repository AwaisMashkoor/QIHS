Closing the Distance

Interactive activity design for inclusive engagement in blended cohorts

Awais Mashkoor
School of Health & Life Sciences · University of the West of Scotland

Social inclusion in learning & assessment Student engagement & partnership 15 min + Q&A
The problem

Two students. One module page. Structurally different experience.

A postgraduate Quality Improvement module delivered to Paisley, London, and distance cohorts via the same AULA page. Same content. Categorically different cognitive engagement.

On-Campus · Monday 10am
In the lecture room
Asks a question mid-session. Discusses a Root Cause Analysis in pairs. Leaves with concepts reinforced through active, social engagement.
"I understood it better when we talked it through."
Distance Learner · Tuesday 10pm
After a clinical shift
Watches the recording. No friction, no discussion, no moment where the content pushes back. Same slides. Entirely different cognitive experience.
"I watched it but I'm not sure I've actually done anything with it."
This is not a technology problem — it is a design problem
The design framework

Three questions. Every activity. Every week.

A structured approach grounded in learning outcomes — asking what each activity actually requires of the student, not what content it covers.

1
What cognitive work does this activity actually require?
Is the student remembering, applying, analysing, or evaluating? Not what the content is — what the student must do with it. Passive reading and active classification are categorically different.
Cognitive Demand
2
Does the activity match what the assessment actually demands?
If the assessment requires critically appraising a QI methodology, weekly activities should rehearse that exact cognitive move — not simply expose students to related content.
Assessment Alignment
3
Can a distance learner do this meaningfully without being present?
If the answer is no — if the activity only works in a room — it is not an inclusive activity. The constraint of distance is the most useful design test.
Inclusion Test
If it fails question three, redesign it. The distance learner at 10pm deserves the same cognitive experience as the student who just left the lecture room.

The unexpected finding
Designed for distance learners.
Improved the experience for everyone.
Activities were embedded in the shared AULA environment, accessible to the full cohort. MEQ data from the distance learning cohort showed the redesigned activities produced strong engagement across nearly every dimension — with one telling exception.
MEQ — Distance Learning Cohort · 2025–26
Nine items at Definitely Agree. One at Agree: community and belonging — the social dimension that activities alone cannot fully replicate. The cognitive gap closed. The relational gap narrowed but persists.
Distance learning cohort · n = 1 · Likert 1–5
📍
The Cognitive Gap Closed
Engagement, depth, feedback quality, organisation — all at ceiling. The interactive activities gave the distance learner the same cognitive experience as on-campus peers.
🎯
Whole-Cohort Benefit
On-campus students engaged with the same activities and reported equivalent gains. Designing for the margins raised the floor for everyone.
Unexpected Finding
💡
The Honest Limit
Community and belonging scored 4, not 5. Activities can equalise cognitive engagement — but the social dimension needs deliberate, additional design. That is the next frontier.
You don't need new technology. You need new design questions. These four steps work in any discipline, with tools your institution already has.
01

Identify cognitive demand

What is the student actually doing — remembering, applying, analysing, evaluating?

02

Map against assessment

Are activities rehearsing the same cognitive move the assessment requires?

03

Apply the inclusion test

Can a student do this meaningfully at 10pm, alone, without being in the room?

04

Build a shared environment

Same interactive experience regardless of attendance mode, embedded in your existing VLE.

Inclusive activity design is not a specialist accommodation — it is good teaching practice.
