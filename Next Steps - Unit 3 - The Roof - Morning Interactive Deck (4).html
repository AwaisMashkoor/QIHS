The roof: the part of the house you can’t see

What the House of Care calls it

“Organisational processes, policies, systems and arrangements.”

The four parts, in plain words

  • Processes — how things get done: test results sent out before your appointment
  • Policies — the rules: who can see your information
  • Systems — the tools: records that can be shared
  • Arrangements — how it’s organised: appointments coordinated, teams with time to plan

An everyday example

The wiring and the pipes in your home. You never see them — until the lights go out, or water comes through the ceiling.

You never see the roof from the waiting room. You only notice it when it leaks.

1948The NHS begins — a national health service, free at the point of use.
1968Local-authority social work responsibilities consolidated in Scotland.
Decades apartDifferent histories, responsibilities, funding and rules — then computer systems built inside each.
2014 · 2025Integration, then the digital record: attempts to bridge the “fault line”.
The computers are a symptom. The split is older than the computers.

The most important explanation of the morning. Without it, students leave thinking the computers are simply bad. The point: this split did not begin with computers. The NHS developed as a national health service from 1948; social work responsibilities developed through local government and were consolidated in Scotland by the Social Work (Scotland) Act 1968. The two grew with different accountabilities, funding, eligibility rules and professional traditions — so when digital systems arrived, they were built inside organisations that were already separate. And there are many systems inside each world, not one each. When Scotland proposed integration, its own papers called the divide a “fault line”. Now the 2014 Act, the IJBs and the 2025 digital-record duty make sense as bridges across one old divide. If you came through First Steps: this is Beveridge and the welfare state, landing where it explains something. Product names, only if students recognise them from placement: TrakCare, Clinical Portal and HEPMA on the NHS side; Liquidlogic, CareSys and CM2000 on the council side. The CHI number is worth one sentence: the NHS in Scotland identifies each person by it; council systems grew up separately and don’t always use it. For London: many English areas have local shared care records, and the same boundary problem still appears at the edges.

What happens when information has to cross the line?

A story about one word

MondayAgnes, 74, is admitted to a medical ward. She tells the nurse she once had a bad reaction to penicillin. It is written in her ward notes.
FridayShe goes home. The discharge letter is written in a hurry from a template. The allergy box is left empty.
Next weekHer GP sees her for a chest infection. The GP’s record has no allergy on it — she had never mentioned it at the practice. The GP prescribes an antibiotic from the penicillin family.
Two days laterAgnes has a reaction and is back in hospital.
So…The allergy was known in one setting — and did not survive the transition.

A made-up story, built from the kind of thing patient-safety reviews describe.

Tell it slowly, one step at a time. Pause before the last line. Ask: where did it break? (The discharge letter — the handover.) Then the move that matters: do not stop at individual blame. Ask what process allowed critical information to be lost — a rushed template, a box that could be left empty, no check before the letter went. That is how safety reviews think, and it is how students should think in their reports. The story is invented and the slide says so. Keep the point: information failure is not an IT inconvenience, it is a safety issue, and it happens at transitions. The next slide turns the story into evidence. You come back to allergies at 11:35 — medicines and allergies are among the first things people can now see on MyCare.scot.

Her story isn’t rare

A global priority

When the World Health Organization launched its challenge Medication Without Harm in 2017, it named three priorities. One was transitions of care — for example, being admitted to hospital, or discharged from it.

Why such a wide range — a quarter, or four-fifths? Use day one’s questions: compared with what? Over how long? What else could explain it?
Information is most likely to get lost at the handover — exactly where the roof has to hold.

Turn the invented story into evidence in one move: Agnes is made up; her story isn’t rare. The World Health Organization’s technical report on medication safety in transitions of care (2019) cites a review of studies: at discharge, between 25% and 80% of patients had at least one medication discrepancy, or a failure to pass on medication changes made in hospital. Then the Level 8 move — ask why the range is so wide. Draw out: studies count different things (any mismatch, or only ones that matter?), check in different ways (a pharmacist going through every medicine finds more than a quick audit), in different countries, hospitals and patient groups (older people on many medicines have more). So a careful student does not write “80% of discharges have errors”; they write “studies report between a quarter and four-fifths, depending on how mismatches are defined and measured” — and the handover is the risk point either way. A mismatch is not always harm — many are caught or don’t matter — but each one is information that didn’t travel. WHO’s Medication Without Harm challenge, launched on 29 March 2017, named three priorities: high-risk situations, polypharmacy and transitions of care. Then stop on the question for the break: what would stop the next Agnes? Don’t answer it — it is the first thing after the break, and the fourth of the four ideas, good handovers are safe care, is this slide.

Unit 3Four ideasRoof: processes and systems.RoofProcessesPoliciesSystemsArrangements
What would stop the next Agnes? Four ideas

Four ideas about information

Information must follow the person
Relevant information has to reach each person who needs it — not stay behind in the building they have just left.
For Richard: his medication changes need to reach the people prescribing and monitoring them; the housing problem needs to reach the people who can act on it — before they need to act.
Systems need to connect
The technical word is interoperability: systems that can share information and understand it.
For Richard: NHS and council systems often don’t share information directly. Until they do, people have to make the connection — a phone call, a meeting, a shared plan.
What you record matters
What you write is part of the care. The next person can only act on what’s there.
For Richard: “non-compliant with meds” tells the next person nothing. “Finding the changes to his diet and routine hard; the ward is supporting him with his medication” tells them where to start.
Good handovers are safe care
Discharge is a high-risk transition. Care, and the information about it, has to pass across in one go.
For Richard: what each person needs has to pass across when he leaves the ward — and arrive before anyone needs to act on it.
A shared record makes information visible. It doesn’t decide who acts on it. Information needs to travel — and responsibility needs to travel with it.

Start with the break question: what would stop the next Agnes? Take two or three answers, then show that each one is one of these four ideas. Give this block room — about 12 minutes. It is the intellectual core of the morning: why information fragments, why that matters, how it should travel, and how professionals themselves shape information quality. Teach each idea in two or three minutes: say it, ask for an example from placement or their own lives, then click for the Richard line. Name interoperability once, in plain words, and don’t test it. Note the first idea says relevant information to each person who needs it — not everything to everyone; the next slide makes that explicit. Slow down on the third idea: it is the only one every student controls personally, from their first shift. The band stops this becoming a technology lecture — a record can show the damp; it can’t decide who phones housing. Idea one said relevant information. So should everyone see everything?

Sharing information well

The right information→to the right person→for the right reason→at the right time

The housing officer

Needs to know the housing problem is urgent, and what has to be fixed for Richard to come home safely.

Probably doesn’t need the details of his diagnosis.

Those treating him after discharge

Need to know what changed in hospital — his medicines, his follow-up, and what happened during his admission.

His daughter

Being family doesn’t mean seeing everything. What she’s told should be Richard’s choice — and she needs enough to support him.

Good integration is not maximum sharing. It is appropriate sharing.

The Caldicott principles, which guide how health and care services handle confidential information, say the duty to share information for someone’s care is as important as the duty to protect their confidentiality — and to use only what is necessary.

Everything so far this morning leans towards sharing. Without this slide a student could fairly conclude that integrated care means everyone sees everything — which is wrong. The judgement is: share what is relevant and necessary, not everything with everybody. Schizophrenia carries stigma, so this is not abstract for Richard. Ask about the housing officer — do they need his diagnosis, or that he has been unwell, is due to go home, and needs the heating fixed first? One spoken caveat, no more: the person’s preference is central, but information can sometimes be shared without consent where the law allows — for example, to protect someone from serious harm. No information-law lecture; one line in small print is enough, and every NHS board in Scotland has a Caldicott Guardian whose job is exactly this balance. End with the bridge: where do Richard’s own choice and control sit in all this? That is this afternoon.

Unit 3How we’d knowRoof: processes and policies.RoofProcessesPoliciesSystemsArrangements
Money is tight. So how would we know the roof is working?

How would we know Richard’s care is safe and good?

In policy documents this is called quality: how good and safe care is — and how we know.

Scrutiny and improvement

Healthcare Improvement Scotland supports improvement and provides scrutiny and assurance of healthcare. The Care Inspectorate inspects and regulates care services and social work.

There are standards

The Health and Social Care Standards set out what people in Scotland should expect — written from the person’s point of view: “My support, my life.” They rest on five principles: dignity and respect; compassion; be included; responsive care and support; wellbeing.

When things go wrong

Incidents are reported, reviewed and learned from. When an unintended or unexpected incident results in death or harm, as the law defines it, the organisation must be open with the person — the duty of candour.

The catch

Each service can meet its own targets while nobody looks at whether Richard is doing better overall. Knowing his care is good means measuring what matters to him: living at home, tenancy kept, fewer avoidable crises and admissions, his daughter supported.

In England: the Care Quality Commission regulates services and NICE sets guidelines.

Say the word quality once — it is what policy documents call how good and safe care is, and how we know — then use the plain question. Keep this light — one sentence per box. Healthcare Improvement Scotland’s role is wider than inspection: improvement support, scrutiny and assurance. The duty of candour is precise: it applies to unintended or unexpected incidents that result in death or harm as defined in the legislation — not to everything that goes wrong. The five principles of the Standards are worth reading aloud: they come back this afternoon as the values of person-centred care. The catch is the Level 8 point and links back to day one’s person outcomes versus system outcomes. For Agnes: her incident would be reported and reviewed, and she would be told. Richard’s roof

Processes

Now: the ward has identified his housing problems and requested housing input; a referral to the wellbeing hub has been suggested.
Strong roof: his discharge planned with housing involved, and information reaching each person before they need to act on it.

Policies

Now: the case doesn’t tell us what has been agreed about sharing his information, or what his daughter can be told.
Strong roof: appropriate sharing — the right information to each person, and Richard’s choice about what his daughter is told.

Systems

Now: his information sits in several places, across two worlds — some named in the case, others (his GP, a mental health team) assumed.
Strong roof: records that connect — and, until they do, a phone call, a meeting, a shared plan.

Arrangements

Now: the case does not identify a named person coordinating across all the services involved.
Strong roof: a named lead, clear accountability, and agreed resources across the relevant partners.
How would we know it’s working? Measures that matter to him: living at home, tenancy kept, fewer avoidable crises, his daughter supported.

Straight after the activity: each group reads out its fix, and you place it on a column — which part of the roof does it repair? Most fixes repair more than one part; let them argue it, because that argument is the learning. Each “Now” is worded to separate fact from inference — the known-versus-assumed habit again. The case doesn’t say nobody is in charge; it doesn’t identify a named coordinator. It doesn’t say his information isn’t shared; it doesn’t tell us what has been agreed. His GP and a mental health team are our assumptions, not the case’s. Note the arrangements column says agreed resources across the relevant partners — not a joint budget for housing, because which housing functions are delegated varies locally, as we taught on day one. Finish on the band: how would we know it’s working? Measures that matter to him — the same question each group answered for its own fix.

Glossary — the roof

Plain English, one line each.

Records and information

  • Record / notes — what a service writes down about a person. Every service keeps its own.
  • CHI number — the number the NHS in Scotland uses to identify each person.
  • Discharge letter — the letter from the hospital to the GP when someone goes home: what happened, and what changed.
  • Handover — the moment care, and the information about it, passes from one team to another.
  • Transition of care — when a person moves between settings, such as being admitted to hospital or going home.
  • Interoperability — computer systems that can share information and understand it.
  • Digital integrated care record — digital records that draw together a person’s health and social care information so it can be shared securely. Scotland now has a legal duty to make this possible.
  • MyCare.scot — Scotland's new app for people to see some of their own health and care information.

The rest of the roof

  • The roof’s four parts — processes (how things get done), policies (the rules), systems (the tools, such as records) and arrangements (how it’s organised: who’s in charge, the money). The House of Care’s words; the plain meanings are ours.
  • Governance — who's in charge, what the rules are, and how decisions get made. The word policy documents use.
  • Quality — how good and safe care is, and how we know. The word policy documents use.
  • Accountability — who has to answer for a decision, or for what went wrong.
  • Funding gap — the difference between the money a body expects to need and the money it expects to have.
  • Integration joint board (IJB) — in most Scottish areas, the body that plans how delegated NHS and council resources are used for integrated functions, and directs the partners who deliver them.
  • Integrated care board (ICB) — in England, the body that plans and pays for local NHS services.
  • Healthcare Improvement Scotland — inspects and improves NHS care in Scotland.
  • Care Inspectorate — inspects care services and social work in Scotland.
  • Health and Social Care Standards — what people in Scotland should expect from care, written from their point of view.
  • Appropriate sharing — the right information, to the right person, for the right reason, at the right time.
  • Caldicott Guardian — the senior person in each NHS board responsible for protecting confidentiality and enabling appropriate sharing.
  • Implementation gap — the space between what policy says should happen and what routinely happens in practice.
  • Duty of candour — the legal duty for organisations to be open with people when care has caused harm.
